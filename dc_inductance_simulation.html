💡 Why Does Inductance Act Like Inertia?

In mechanics, Newton's first law states that an object with mass resists any change in its velocity ($F = m \cdot a$). An inductor does the exact same thing for electric current!

When current changes ($\frac{dI}{dt} \ne 0$), the changing magnetic flux induces a counter-voltage (Back-EMF $\mathcal{E}_L = -L \frac{dI}{dt}$). This voltage directly opposes the change, forcing current to change gradually rather than instantly.

⚖️ Mechanical ↔ Electrical Analogy

Electrical Quantity Mechanical Equivalent
Current $I(t)$ Velocity $v(t)$
Inductance $L$ Mass / Inertia $m$
Battery Voltage $V$ Applied Force $F$
Resistance $R$ Friction / Drag $b$
Back-EMF $-L \frac{dI}{dt}$ Inertial Force $-m \frac{dv}{dt}$
Magnetic Energy $\frac{1}{2} L I^2$ Kinetic Energy $\frac{1}{2} m v^2$

⏱️ The Time Constant (τ = L / R)

The time constant $\tau = \frac{L}{R}$ dictates how fast the circuit responds:

  • At $t = 1\tau$: Current reaches 63.2% of its maximum value ($I_{max} = \frac{V}{R}$).
  • At $t = 3\tau$: Current reaches 95.0%.
  • At $t = 5\tau$: Current reaches 99.3% (considered steady state).

Increasing $L$ adds more "inertia", stretching $\tau$. Increasing $R$ causes energy to dissipate faster, shrinking $\tau$.
